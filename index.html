Australia’s Silent Extinction

How climate change, habitat destruction and invasive species are pushing Australia’s wildlife toward collapse.

A crisis hidden in plain sight

Australia has one of the highest extinction rates in the world. Hundreds of native species now face severe threats caused by bushfires, land clearing, invasive predators and climate change.

This visualisation explores where endangered species live, what threatens them, and whether conservation efforts are enough to prevent irreversible biodiversity loss.

Where species are disappearing

Endangered species are not evenly distributed across Australia. Biodiversity hotspots overlap heavily with regions experiencing urban expansion, deforestation and climate stress.

The growing pressures on wildlife

Over the past two decades, environmental pressures affecting Australian wildlife have intensified dramatically. Habitat destruction remains the dominant threat, while climate change and bushfires have accelerated rapidly.

Species on the brink

Some Australian species now survive in alarmingly small numbers. For several critically endangered animals, population collapse has accelerated due to habitat fragmentation, invasive predators and extreme climate events.

What is driving extinction?

Australian wildlife faces interconnected pressures. Habitat destruction and invasive species remain the largest drivers of biodiversity collapse, while climate change increasingly amplifies every existing threat.

The aftermath of megafires

Australia's recent megafire seasons have devastated fragile ecosystems. As bushfire intensity increases, wildlife population decline accelerates sharply across multiple regions.

Fragile ecosystems under pressure

Biodiversity loss is not isolated to individual species. Entire Australian ecosystems are facing mounting pressure, with rainforests, coral reefs and eucalyptus forests now experiencing critical levels of ecological stress.

Regional biodiversity pressure

Extinction pressure varies significantly across Australia. Amphibians and mammals now face especially severe threats in densely populated and climate-sensitive regions.

Where conservation funding flows

Australia’s conservation response remains uneven. While habitat restoration and bushfire recovery receive significant investment, long-term biodiversity monitoring and species recovery programs continue to face funding gaps.

Population collapse across decades

Wildlife decline rarely happens suddenly. Across multiple Australian species, population loss has accelerated steadily over the past two decades as ecological pressures continue to intensify.

A crisis decades in the making

Australia’s biodiversity collapse did not emerge overnight. Decades of environmental pressure, habitat destruction and climate disruption have progressively reshaped ecosystems across the continent.
